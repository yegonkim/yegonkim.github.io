## Background

### SGD and Bayesian Inference

SGD and Bayesian inference are closely related: the (stochastic) outcome of SGD, when run on an objective function $L$, can *roughly* be interpreted as a sample from the Gibbs posterior over parameters:

$$
\omega(\theta) \propto \omega_0(\theta) \exp(-\beta L(\theta))
$$

where $\beta$ is determined by learning rate, batch size, etc., and the prior $\omega_0$ is determined by weight initialization, weight decay, etc. [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04289)
[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_gradient_Langevin_dynamics)

There is a parameter-function map $\mathcal M: \Theta \to \mathcal F$, which maps parameters $\theta$ to functions $f$. The prior over parameters $\omega_0$ induces a prior over functions $w_0$ via the pushforward measure: $w_0 = \mathcal M_\# \omega_0$.
If the objective $L(\theta)$ only depends on the function $f$ that the parameter $\theta$ represents, then one can show that the induced distribution over functions, $w = \mathcal M_\# \omega$, is also of the Gibbs form:

$$
w(f) \propto w_0(f) \exp(-\beta L(f))
.
\tag{1}
$$

There are arguments for the case that $w_0$ is roughly the simplicity prior over functions [[3]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.08522).

### Offline vs Streaming SGD

Let's consider the sequential prediction setting, where we have a sequence of bits $x_1, x_2, \ldots$ and we want to predict $x_t$ given $x_{<t}$. A Bayesian would maintain a posterior over predictors $\pi$:

$$
w_t(\pi) \propto w_0(\pi) \prod_{j=1}^t \pi(x_j \mid x_{<j} ).
$$

**Offline SGD.**
The Bayesian approach is equivalent to running SGD on the log-likelihood objective

$$
L_t(\pi) = -\sum_{j=1}^t \log \pi(x_j \mid x_{<j}).
\tag{2}
$$

**Streaming SGD.**
In practice, it is too expensive to run SGD from scratch on the whole history at every new time step $t$.
What we often do instead is to run a single step of gradient descent with the new data point $x_t$, on the parameter $\theta_{t-1}$ from time $t-1$. This is called streaming SGD.
We are effectively running SGD through time $t$, so it doesn't have an "equilibrium" distribution at any given time step.
We thus can no longer "pushforward" to the function space; there is no distribution to pushforward from.
I think we have to go into the messy details of gradient descent in the parameter space, to understand the behavior of streaming SGD in practice.



### Exponential weights

There is a class of algorithms called exponential weights, which allows for regret that grows favorably (e.g. sublinearly), with respect to some choice of loss function $\ell$. The general recipe is that if we have an unbiased estimate $\hat \ell_t$ of the loss $\ell_t$ at each time step, we perform the following updates:

$$
w_t(\pi) \propto w_{t-1}(\pi) \exp(-\eta_t \hat \ell_t(\pi))
$$

Bayesian inference is a special case of exponential weights where $\ell$ is the negative log-likelihood:

$$
w_t(\pi) \propto w_{t-1}(\pi) \pi(x_t \mid x_{<t})^{\eta_t}
$$

In fact, we know that Bayes achieves finite regret almost surely.

### Policy Optimization

So far, we have been talking about supervised learning.
The same ideas transfer to reinforcement learning, by replacing the log-likelihood objective with an unbiased estimate of the expected return.

Let's consider the bandit setting, where we have a sequence of actions and percepts $a_1 e_1 a_2 e_2 \dots$, and at every time step $t$, we want to choose an action $a_t$ that maximizes the immediate reward $r_t$.
The percept $e_t$ is generated by some environment $\mu$ that sees the history $h_{<t} a_t$.
Our objective function at time $t$ is hence

$$
J_t(\pi) = \sum_a
\pi(a \mid h_{<t})
\mathbb E[r_t \mid h_{<t}a].
$$

The problem is that we only observe the reward corresponding to the action we actually take. Suppose our current behavior policy is $\pi^{(t)}$, and we sample
$
a_t \sim \pi^{(t)}(\cdot \mid h_{<t})
$,
after which we observe reward $r_t$.
For any counterfactual policy $\pi$, we can construct an unbiased importance-sampling estimate of its expected reward:

$$
\hat J_t(\pi) = 
r_t
\frac{\pi(a_t \mid h_{<t})}
{\pi^{(t)}(a_t \mid h_{<t})}.
\tag{3}
$$

Indeed,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{a_t \sim \pi^{(t)}}
\left[
r_t
\frac{\pi(a_t \mid h_{<t})}
{\pi^{(t)}(a_t \mid h_{<t})}
\right]
&=
\sum_a
\pi^{(t)}(a \mid h_{<t})
\frac{\pi(a \mid h_{<t})}
{\pi^{(t)}(a \mid h_{<t})}
\mathbb E[r_t \mid h_{<t}a]
\\
&=
\sum_a
\pi(a \mid h_{<t})
\mathbb E[r_t \mid h_{<t}a]
\\
&=
J_t(\pi).
\end{aligned}
$$

**EXP4.**
Now that we have an unbiased estimate of our objective function, we can do exponential weights to update our distribution over policies:

$$
w_t(\pi)
\propto
w_{t-1}(\pi)
\exp\left(
\eta_t
\hat J_t(\pi)
\right).
\tag{4}
$$

This is also called the EXP4 algorithm, which achieves favorable regret bounds. 

**Off-policy REINFORCE.**
Due to equation 1, we can also view equation 4 as the result of performing SGD on the loss function

$$
L_t(\pi) = -\sum_{j=1}^t \hat J_j(\pi),
$$

which is exactly the off-policy REINFORCE algorithm.
Just as Bayesian inference $\approx$ offline SGD, we see that EXP4 $\approx$ off-policy REINFORCE.

**On-policy REINFORCE.**
Like in supervised learning, running SGD from scratch at every new time step is too expensive.
We have an additional complication in reinforcement learning: the importance sampling estimator introduces variance when $\pi^{(t)}$ is different from $\pi$.

Suppose that our policies are parameterized as $\pi_\theta$. Taking the gradient of $\hat J_t$ gives

$$
r_t
\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid h_{<t})}
{\pi^{(t)}(a_t \mid h_{<t})}
\nabla_\theta
\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid h_{<t}).
\tag{5}
$$

If we evaluate this gradient at the current behavior policy, so that $\pi_\theta = \pi^{(t)}$, then the importance ratio is $1$, and we recover the usual REINFORCE estimator:

$$
r_t
\nabla_\theta
\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid h_{<t}).
$$

Thus REINFORCE is using the same underlying objective as off-policy REINFORCE, but like streaming SGD, it is taking a single gradient step per time $t$.

| | Supervised learning | Reinforcement learning |
|---|---|---|
| Offline gradient | Offline SGD | Off-policy REINFORCE |
| Exponential weights | Bayesian inference | EXP4 |
| Online gradient | Streaming SGD  | On-policy REINFORCE |

For both SL and RL, we have seen that the offline gradient algorithms are approximating the exponential weights algorithm, while online gradient algorithms do not have a clear function-space analogue.


**Episodic RL.**
The same discussion extends naturally to a sequence of concatenated episodes. Let $t_k$ denote the time step at which episode $k$ starts. For a policy $\pi$, define

$$
J_k(\pi)
=
\mathbb E_{\tau_k\sim \mu^\pi(\cdot\mid \tau_{<k})}[R(\tau_k)].
$$

If episode $k$ is generated by the behavior policy $\pi^{(k)}$, then an unbiased estimator is

$$
\hat J_k(\pi)
=
R(\tau_k)
\frac{\pi (\tau_k \mid \tau_{<k})}
{\pi^{(k)}(\tau_k \mid \tau_{<k})}
$$

where we abused the notation a bit for $\pi (\tau_k \mid \tau_{<k})$ to mean $\prod_{i=t_k}^{t_{k+1}-1} \pi(a_i \mid h_{<i}) $.
Thus, each episode can be treated as one round of a bandit, with exponential weights giving the off-policy analogue and an on-policy gradient step recovering episodic REINFORCE.


### Neural network as a mixture of functions

One way to study streaming SGD under the function space framework is to assume that a neural network is a weighted mixture of functions,

$$
\pi(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty w_i \pi_i(x_t \mid x_{<t})
$$

and that performing gradient descent is equivalent to updating the weights $w_i$.
Since $(w_i)_{i=1}^\infty$ lies on the probability simplex, a natural way to update them is via mirror descent.
This gives us

$$
w_i^{(t)} \propto w_i^{(t-1)} \exp
\left(
\eta_t \frac{\pi_i(x_t \mid x_{<t})}{\pi^{(t-1)}(x_t \mid x_{<t})}
\right)
.
$$

Now let's consider the RL setting with the same mixture architecture:

$$
\pi(a_t \mid h_{<t}) =
\sum_{i=1}^\infty w_i \pi_i(a_t \mid h_{<t}).
$$

If we update the weights using mirror descent on $\hat J_t$, we obtain

$$
w_i^{(t)}
\propto
w_i^{(t-1)}
\exp\left(
\eta_t r_t
\frac{
\pi_i(a_t \mid h_{<t})
}{
\pi^{(t-1)}(a_t \mid h_{<t})
}
\right),
$$

which is exactly the EXP4 update.

So for this mixture architecture, the distinction between on-policy REINFORCE and EXP4 disappears.
I think that, if we want to understand the behavior of online gradient-based algorithms in practice, we eventually have to get into the messy details of gradient descent in the parameter space.

*Side note: My opinion is that analyzing online gradient algorithm is not as important as analyzing the offline methods, because powerful algorithms with lots of available compute will converge toward the latter.*

## First Problem

The first problem is showing that the posterior mass tends to concentrate on "optimal" policies (where optimality is defined in some sense as we discuss below) and that, out of those policies, the ones with high prior weight tend to retain high posterior weight.


### Bayesian Sequence Prediction

Bayesian inference is a good starting point for understanding the problem, since it is also an exponential weights algorithm like EXP4, but one that we are much more familiar with.

Consider the hypothesis class $\mathcal M$ of l.s.c. semimeasures. The universal semimeasure $M$ is in our hypothesis class. If the true distribution is a computable measure $P \neq M$, then $M$ is not optimal in the sense that $\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel M) > \mathrm{KL}(P\parallel P) = 0$. Still, $\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel M)$ is finite, due to the universality of $M$.
With the log-likelihood objective

$$
L_t(\pi) = -\sum_{j=1}^t \log \pi(x_j \mid x_{<j}),
$$

and a decaying $\beta_t = o(1)$, we have $\lim_{t \to \infty} \beta_t (L_t(M) - L_t(P)) = 0$ almost surely. We can then show

$$
\lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{w_t(M)}{w_t(P)} = \frac{w_0(M)}{w_0(P)},
$$

which is the nicest result we can possibly hope for.

Put into words, the intuition is that the finite errors made by $M$ eventually get *forgotten* (due to our choice of $\beta_t=o(1)$), and thus $M$ and $P$ are treated as if they were equally "optimal" in the long run, with their posterior odds reverting to their prior odds.

### But this isn't the standard Bayesian update

However, we have $\beta_t = 1$ in the standard Bayesian update.
Things become more complicated in this case, because we no longer forget the finite errors made by $M$.
Interestingly, if we are talking about the universal semimeasure $M$ built with a prior identical to the prior $w_0$ over our hypothesis class $\mathcal M$, then

$$\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel M) \le -\log w_0(P),$$

and therefore

$$
\lim_{t \to \infty} \mathbb E \left[ \log \frac{w_t(M)}{w_t(P)} \right]
=
\log \frac{w_0(M)}{w_0(P)}
-
\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel M)
\ge
\log w_0(M).
$$

In other words, if $P$ has very low prior weight, $M$ makes more errors, and these two effects cancel out so that $M$ is not favored over $P$ in expectation.
**Therefore, I see no reason to expect our hypothesis, or even softened versions of it, to hold in the standard Bayesian update case.**
(Unless, we are talking about $P$ that satisfies $\mathrm{KL}(P \parallel M) \ll -\log w_0(P)$, but this is just assuming what we want to show.)

### EXP4

Let's now consider the EXP4 algorithm, which is also an exponential weights algorithm, with importance-weighted reward estimates as the loss:

$$
L_t(\pi)
=
-\sum_{j=1}^t \hat J_j(\pi).
$$

For the analysis below, define
$G_t(\pi)=-L_t(\pi)$,
and let

$$
w_i^{(t+1)}
\propto
w_i^{(0)}
\exp\left(
\beta_t
G_t(\pi_i)
\right).
\tag{6}
$$

We will mostly consider

$$
\beta_t=t^{-b},
\qquad
0<b<1,
$$

so that $\beta_t\to0$ while $t\beta_t\to\infty$.

#### Regret bound

Let $q_t(a)=\sum_i w_i^{(t)}\pi_i(a\mid h_{<t})$ be the action distribution recommended by the current expert mixture.
Suppose we add explicit uniform exploration,

$$
\pi^{(t)}(a)
=
(1-\gamma_t)q_t(a)
+
\frac{\gamma_t}{K},
\tag{7}
$$

where $K$ is the number of actions.

Recall that for any policy $\pi_i$, we defined

$$
J_t(\pi_i)
=
\sum_a
\pi_i(a\mid h_{<t})
\mathbb E[
r_t
\mid
h_{<t}a
].
$$

Assume that the policy class contains the optimal policy $\pi^\ast_\mu$.
Define the regret of a policy $\pi$ by

$$
\mathcal R_T(\pi)
=
\sum_{t=1}^T
\left[
J_t(\pi^\ast_\mu)-J_t(\pi)
\right].
$$

For the EXP4 behavior policy $\pi$, we have the guarantee that

$$
\mathbb E[
\mathcal R_T(\pi)
]
\lesssim
\frac{
\log(1/w_\ast^{(0)})
}{
\beta_T
}
+
K\sum_{t=1}^T\beta_t
+
\sum_{t=1}^T\gamma_t,
$$

where expectation is over the randomness of history.

For the power schedules

$$
\beta_t=t^{-b},
\qquad
\gamma_t=t^{-g},
$$

this becomes

$$
\mathbb E[
\mathcal R_T(\pi)
]
=
O\left(
T^b\log\frac1{w_\ast^{(0)}}
+
KT^{1-b}
+
T^{1-g}
\right).
$$

Thus,
$
0<b<1
$
and
$
0<g<1
$
is enough to obtain

$$
\mathbb E[
\mathcal R_T(\pi)
]
=
o(T).
$$

The standard minimax EXP4 scale that balances $T^b$ and $T^{1-b}$ corresponds to $b=1/2$:

$$
\beta_t = t^{-1/2}.
$$

This gives the familiar $\sqrt T$-scale regret.


#### Decomposing the importance-weighted estimator

EXP4 does not observe $J_t(\pi_i)$ directly. It observes only $(a_t,r_t)$ and uses

$$
\hat J_t(\pi_i)
=
r_t
\frac{
\pi_i(a_t\mid h_{<t})
}{
\pi^{(t)}(a_t)
}.
$$

We can split the estimation error into two martingale-noise terms:

$$
\hat J_t(\pi_i)-J_t(\pi_i)
=
\xi_t^{\mathrm{reward}}(\pi_i)
+
\xi_t^{\mathrm{action}}(\pi_i),
$$

where

$$
\xi_t^{\mathrm{reward}}(\pi_i)
=
\frac{
\pi_i(a_t\mid h_{<t})
}{
\pi^{(t)}(a_t)
}
\left[
r_t-\bar r_t(a_t)
\right],
$$

and

$$
\xi_t^{\mathrm{action}}(\pi_i)
=
\frac{
\pi_i(a_t\mid h_{<t})
\bar r_t(a_t)
}{
\pi^{(t)}(a_t)
}
-
J_t(\pi_i).
$$

The first term is stochastic reward noise. The second is action-sampling/importance-weighting noise.

Both have conditional expectation zero:

$$
\mathbb E[
\xi_t^{\mathrm{reward}}(\pi_i)
\mid h_{<t}
]
=
0,
$$

and

$$
\mathbb E[
\xi_t^{\mathrm{action}}(\pi_i)
\mid h_{<t}
]
=
0.
$$

Hence

$$
Z_t(\pi_i)
:=
\sum_{s=1}^t
\left[
\hat J_s(\pi_i)-J_s(\pi_i)
\right]
$$

is a martingale.

For two policies,

$$
M_t(\pi_i,\pi_j)
=
Z_t(\pi_j)-Z_t(\pi_i)
$$

is also a martingale, and

$$
G_t(\pi_j)
-
G_t(\pi_i)
=
\mathcal R_t(\pi_i)-\mathcal R_t(\pi_j)
+
M_t(\pi_i,\pi_j).
\tag{8}
$$

Thus

$$
\text{estimated cumulative reward gap}
=
\text{regret drift}
+
\text{martingale noise}.
$$


#### Exploration controls the martingale variance

The importance ratio can become very large if the behavior policy $\pi^{(t)}$ assigns very little probability to the action chosen.

With uniform exploration in Equation 7,
$
\pi^{(t)}(a)\ge \gamma_t/K.
$
Since rewards lie in $[0,1]$,

$$
\mathbb E[
\hat J_t(\pi_i)^2
\mid
h_{<t}
]
\le
\sum_a
\frac{\pi_i(a\mid h_{<t})^2}{\pi^{(t)}(a)}
\le
\frac K{\gamma_t}.
$$

Thus, up to constants,

$$
\mathbb E[
(\Delta M_t(\pi_i,\pi_j))^2
\mid h_{<t}
]
\lesssim
\frac K{\gamma_t}.
$$

The martingale enters the log-weight ratio $\log(w_i^{(t+1)}/w_j^{(t+1)})$ as $\beta_tM_t(\pi_i,\pi_j)$. For its noise to vanish, we need
$
\beta_tM_t(\pi_i,\pi_j)\to0
$,
or equivalently
$
{M_t=o(1/\beta_t)}
$.
A simple sufficient condition is

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}
\frac{\beta_t^2}
{\gamma_t}
<
\infty.
$$

For

$$
\beta_t=t^{-b},
\qquad
\gamma_t=t^{-g},
$$

this becomes

$$
\sum_t
t^{-2b+g}
<
\infty,
$$

which holds exactly when

$$
{
2b-g>1.
}
$$

Therefore a particularly useful regime is

$$
{
\frac12<b<1,
\qquad
0<g<2b-1.
}
$$

Under this condition,

$$
\beta_t
M_t(\pi_i,\pi_j)
\to0
\qquad\text{a.s.}
$$

for every fixed pair of policies. Since the policy class is countable, we can take a countable intersection of the probability-one events and obtain the result simultaneously for every pair.

#### The asymptotic weight equation

The decomposition of $G_t(\pi_j)-G_t(\pi_i)$ in Equation 8 gives us a decomposition of the log-weight ratio:

$$
\log
\frac{
w_i^{(t+1)}
}{
w_j^{(t+1)}
}
=
\log
\frac{
w_i^{(0)}
}{
w_j^{(0)}
}
-
\beta_t
\left[
\mathcal R_t(\pi_i)-\mathcal R_t(\pi_j)
\right]
-
\beta_t
M_t(\pi_i,\pi_j).
$$

With the condition $2b-g>1$,

$$
{
\log
\frac{
w_i^{(t+1)}
}{
w_j^{(t+1)}
}
=
\log
\frac{
w_i^{(0)}
}{
w_j^{(0)}
}
-
\beta_t
\left(
\mathcal R_t(\pi_i)-\mathcal R_t(\pi_j)
\right)
+
o(1)
\qquad\text{a.s.}
}
$$

Without the condition, we can still claim

$$
\mathbb E\left[
\log
\frac{
w_i^{(t+1)}
}{
w_j^{(t+1)}}
\right]
=
\log
\frac{w_i^{(0)}
}{
w_j^{(0)}}
-
\beta_t
\left(
\mathbb E[\mathcal R_t(\pi_i)]-\mathbb E[\mathcal R_t(\pi_j)]
\right).
$$


#### What should "optimality" mean?

We define a policy $\pi_i$ to be *weak self-optimizing* in $\mu$ if

$$
\mathcal R_t(\pi_i)
=
o(1/\beta_t),
$$

almost surely when the history $h_{<t}$ is generated by EXP4.

It is called *weak self-optimizing in mean* if $\mathbb E[\mathcal R_t(\pi_i)] = o(1/\beta_t)$.

They respectively imply that the log-weight ratio $\log(w_i^{(t+1)}/w_\ast^{(t+1)})$ converges to its prior value, almost surely (with the condition $2b-g>1$) and in mean.


#### AIXI

We now ask when AIXI is weak self-optimizing along the trajectory generated by EXP4.

Assume the objective is completely myopic, rewards lie in $[0,1]$, the action set has size $K$, and the true environment $\mu$ has prior weight $w(\mu)>0$ in AIXI's Bayesian mixture $\xi$.

Let $\pi_\xi^\ast$ denote the myopically Bayes-optimal policy and define its instantaneous regret

$$
\Delta_t
=
J_t(\pi_\mu^\ast)-J_t(\pi_\xi^\ast).
$$


Define the one-step KL divergence along the EXP4 trajectory by

$$
d_t
=
\sum_a \pi^{(t)}(a)
\operatorname{KL}\left(
\mu(\cdot\mid h_{<t}a)
\,\|\,
\xi(\cdot\mid h_{<t}a)
\right).
$$

By dominance, $\xi^\pi(h_{1:T})\ge w(\mu)\mu^\pi(h_{1:T})$, and the KL chain rule gives

$$
\mathbb E_\mu^\pi\left[
\sum_{t=1}^\infty d_t
\right]
\le
\log\frac1{w(\mu)}.
$$

Since the summands are nonnegative, $\sum_t d_t<\infty$ almost surely as well.

By Pinsker's inequality, bound on instantaneous regret with TV distance, and the action-probability floor of $\pi^{(t)}(a)\ge {\gamma_t}/{K}$, we obtain

$$
d_t
\ge
\frac{\gamma_t}{2K}\Delta_t^2.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\sum_{t=1}^\infty
\gamma_t\Delta_t^2
<
\infty
\qquad \text{a.s.},
$$

and also

$$
\mathbb E\left[
\sum_{t=1}^\infty
\gamma_t\Delta_t^2
\right]
\le
2K\log\frac1{w(\mu)}
<\infty.
$$

By Cauchy-Schwarz,

$$
\mathcal R_T(\pi_\xi^\ast)
=
\sum_{t=1}^T\Delta_t
=
o\left(
\sqrt{
\sum_{t=1}^T\frac1{\gamma_t}
}
\right)
$$

both almost surely and in mean.

Therefore, a sufficient condition for AIXI to be weak self-optimizing, and weak self-optimizing in mean, is

$$
\beta_T
\sqrt{
\sum_{t=1}^T\frac1{\gamma_t}
}
=
O(1).
$$

For the power schedules

$$
\beta_t=t^{-b},
\qquad
\gamma_t=t^{-g},
$$

we have

$$
\sum_{t=1}^T\frac1{\gamma_t}
=
\Theta(T^{1+g}),
$$

so AIXI is weak self-optimizing, both pathwise and in mean, whenever

$$
2b-g\ge1.
$$

Recall that eliminating the importance-weighted martingale noise required the slightly stronger condition

$$
2b-g>1.
$$

Thus the regime already used above is sufficient both for the martingale noise to vanish and for AIXI to be weak self-optimizing along the EXP4-generated trajectory.

### But this isn't EXP4 with $\sqrt T$-scale regret

Although the log-weight ratio in mean doesn't require $2b-g>1$ since the martingale noise vanishes in mean, the condition $2b-g \ge 1$ is still required for AIXI to be weak self-optimizing in mean. If we choose $b=1/2$, then we need $g=0$, which leads to $T$-scale regret due to constant exploration. If we choose $b>1/2$, then we can choose $g>0$ to get sublinear regret, but it cannot be the familiar $\sqrt T$-scale regret. 

#### AIXI with no exploration at $b=1/2$

There's actually a second approach to analyzing the weak self-optimizingness of AIXI. In this analysis we do not need any exploration.

Fix a history $h_{<t}$ and write

$$
w_t(\nu)
=
w(\nu\mid h_{<t})
=
\frac{w(\nu)\nu^\pi(h_{<t})}
{\xi^\pi(h_{<t})}.
$$

Since each environment-wise optimality gap is nonnegative,

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_t(\mu)
\left[
J_t^\mu(\pi_\mu^\ast)-J_t^\mu(\pi_\xi^\ast)
\right]
&\le
\sum_\nu w_t(\nu)
\left[
J_t^\nu(\pi_\nu^\ast)-J_t^\nu(\pi_\xi^\ast)
\right]
\\
&=
\sum_\nu w_t(\nu)J_t^\nu(\pi_\nu^\ast)
-
J_t^\xi(\pi_\xi^\ast).
\end{aligned}
$$

By Bayes optimality of $\pi_\xi^\ast$,

$$
J_t^\xi(\pi_\xi^\ast)\ge J_t^\xi(\pi),
$$

where $\pi$ is the EXP4 behavior policy (without any exploration).
Therefore,

$$
w_t(\mu)
\left[
J_t^\mu(\pi_\mu^\ast)-J_t^\mu(\pi_\xi^\ast)
\right]
\le
\sum_\nu w_t(\nu)
\left[
J_t^\nu(\pi_\nu^\ast)-J_t^\nu(\pi)
\right].
$$

Substituting the posterior expression and multiplying by
$\xi^\pi(h_{<t})/w(\mu)$ gives

$$
\mu^\pi(h_{<t})
\left[
J_t^\mu(\pi_\mu^\ast)-J_t^\mu(\pi_\xi^\ast)
\right]
\le
\frac1{w(\mu)}
\sum_\nu
w(\nu)\nu^\pi(h_{<t})
\left[
J_t^\nu(\pi_\nu^\ast)-J_t^\nu(\pi)
\right].
$$

Summing over $h_{<t}$ yields

$$
\mathbb E_\mu^\pi
\left[
J_t^\mu(\pi_\mu^\ast)-J_t^\mu(\pi_\xi^\ast)
\right]
\le
\frac1{w(\mu)}
\sum_\nu w(\nu)
\mathbb E_\nu^\pi
\left[
J_t^\nu(\pi_\nu^\ast)-J_t^\nu(\pi)
\right].
$$

Finally, summing over $t=1,\ldots,T$ yields

$$
{
\mathbb E_\mu^\pi
\left[
\mathcal R_T^\mu(\pi_\xi^\ast)
\right]
\le
\frac1{w(\mu)}
\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal M}
w(\nu)
\mathbb E_\nu^\pi
\left[
\mathcal R_T^\nu(\pi)
\right].
}
$$

Note that $\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal M}
w(\nu)
\mathbb E_\nu^\pi
\left[
\mathcal R_T^\nu(\pi)
\right]=O(t^{1/2})$ when $\beta_t=t^{-1/2}$ and $\gamma_t=o(t^{-1/2})$.




## Second Problem

The second problem is showing that AIXI really does have high prior weight, among the policies that are weakly self-optimizing in an environment $\mu$.
Actually, I don't find this likely to hold. One can think of modifying AIXI to act weirdly on some finite number of time steps, and it would still be weakly self-optimizing (as the asymptotic behavior is preserved).
And there are myriads of ways to modify AIXI in this way, without increasing its Kolmogorov complexity by much.
Therefore, it would make more sense to think of a family of AIXI-like policies and show that this family occupies a large prior mass among policies that are weakly self-optimizing in $\mu$.
One possible family that I can think of is the "family of policies that are weakly self-optimizing in all computable environments (and not just $\mu$)".
We would also need some assumptions on $\mu$, e.g., $K(\mu)$ being very large (note however that this doens't imply $K(\pi^\ast_\mu)$ is large, so we would need more assumptions).

#### Related problems

There are many other interesting versions of this problem. One can consider the supervised learning setting instead of RL (so we would be showing an analogous result for $M$ instead of AIXI), or consider an i.i.d. setting instead of the continual setting (so $M$ or AIXI can no longer learn in-context from previous data).
The i.i.d. setting presents a distinct flavor of hypotheses, since $M$ or AIXI would no longer be "good" experts.
We therefore have to consider hypotheses that agree with the true distribution $P$ (in SL) or is subgame optimal in $\mu$ (in RL). 
What's interesting is that there is freedom in how the predictor or policy behaves on histories that have very low probability under $P$ or $\mu$ (in the finite data setting) or histories that have zero probability (in the omniscient infinite data setting).
Under what conditions do predictors obtained through Bayesian update or policies obtained through EXP4, behave "like" Solomonoff or AIXI, on these low-probability histories?
